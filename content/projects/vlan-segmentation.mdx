## The problem

The office network was effectively one flat segment. Shared services, administration, production machines and the devices that manage the infrastructure all sat in the same broadcast domain, behind the same broad firewall rule.

Office users and shared services depend on reliable connectivity. When unrelated devices share one logical network, three things become harder:

- **Policy.** A single broad rule has to cover every device, so it permits far more than any one group needs.
- **Troubleshooting.** Broadcast traffic and faults are shared across the whole office, which makes the scope of a problem larger than it should be.
- **Management exposure.** Infrastructure management interfaces sit within reach of ordinary user devices.

VLANs solve the Layer 2 part by creating separate broadcast domains. They do not, on their own, stop traffic from being routed between those domains. That is why the design pairs segmentation with firewall policy and treats the two as one control, not two unrelated tasks.

## Constraints

- **No dedicated project team.** The work had to run alongside day-to-day support in a small IT department, using existing staff and change windows.
- **A live office.** The floor could not be taken offline in one cutover, so devices moved in staged groups with validation after each one.
- **One routing and policy point.** The FortiGate was already the firewall and the natural place to enforce inter-VLAN access. The design had to fit its capacity and availability limits.
- **Management access had to survive.** PVID, uplink and management-address changes can lock an administrator out, so the current state and a rollback path had to be captured before each change.
- **No measured baseline.** No before-and-after performance or security measurement existed, so the page reports the design and the checks rather than a percentage.
- **Publishable detail was limited.** Exact subnets, gateway addresses, policy identifiers and the full port map stay in the internal as-built documentation.

## Architecture

Four VLAN roles were defined, each with its own subnet and a consistent gateway convention.

| VLAN | Purpose | Addressing | Gateway |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 102 | Shared resources | Dedicated `/24` | `.254` |
| 103 | Administration | Dedicated `/24` | `.254` |
| 104 | Production | Dedicated `/24` | `.254` |
| 105 | IT / core infrastructure | Dedicated `/24` | `.254` |

Exact subnet values and interface addresses are deliberately omitted here. They are recorded in the internal as-built documentation, where they belong.

**Component responsibilities:**

- **Managed switches** create the VLANs, assign endpoint ports, set PVIDs and carry the required VLANs across tagged uplinks.
- **FortiGate 60F** provides the VLAN gateway interfaces, routes between subnets and enforces firewall policy.
- **Endpoints** receive the address, mask, gateway and DNS settings for their assigned segment.
- **Documentation** holds the VLAN IDs, subnet and gateway values, port assignments, policy intent and test evidence.

## Implementation approach

1. **Plan the segmentation.** Defined the four roles and the addressing convention before touching a switch. The boundary had to make sense by function, not by where a desk happened to be.
2. **Configure Layer 2.** Created the VLANs on the managed switches, assigned access ports and PVIDs, and allowed the required VLANs across the tagged uplinks.
3. **Configure Layer 3.** Created the VLAN gateway interfaces on the FortiGate and used it as the routing point for every segment.
4. **Apply policy.** Wrote inter-VLAN rules around the services that actually needed to cross a boundary, with a default posture that denies what was not required.
5. **Validate in stages.** Checked addressing, gateway reachability, required services and cross-VLAN paths after each group of devices moved, rather than after the whole cutover.
6. **Document the result.** Recorded purposes, addressing, platform roles and policy intent so the next change starts from a written baseline.

Staging the migration was slower than renumbering everything in one window. It was also far easier to unwind, which matters more when the change affects the network you are using to administer it.

## Business use cases

### Department separation

Administration and production sit in separate broadcast domains. That gives each group a narrower fault domain and a policy boundary that can be described in one sentence.

### IT management protection

Switch and hypervisor management interfaces live in the infrastructure segment. Access to them is restricted to authorised source networks instead of being reachable from every user VLAN.

### Shared service access

User groups can reach designated shared resources without being granted broad access to everything in another segment. The rule describes the service, not the whole subnet.

### Centralised policy enforcement

Inter-VLAN traffic traverses the FortiGate. That gives one place to review, log and change routed access, instead of hunting through switch ACLs and firewall rules that overlap.

### Future expansion

A new device has an obvious home. Its role determines the VLAN and subnet, and the port assignment follows the documented convention rather than an improvised one.

## Security and operational considerations

- **A VLAN is not a firewall.** Segmentation creates broadcast domains. Inter-VLAN access still has to be denied or permitted explicitly.
- **Least privilege applies inside the LAN too.** Permit the required source, destination and service; deny the rest. A flat "trusted internal network" assumption undoes most of the benefit.
- **Management needs its own path.** Restricting management access is only useful if administrators still have a reliable way in, including a console fallback.
- **Change the management plane carefully.** Switch management VLANs, PVIDs, uplinks and management addresses can lock you out. Capture the current state and keep a rollback path.
- **Test the protocol, not just the host.** ICMP reachability does not prove an application is available. Check the actual TCP port the service uses.
- **Policy order matters.** In first-match systems, the position of a rule is part of its meaning. A confidently named rule can still do the opposite of its label.

The checks that still need a written, dated record are the denied-path tests and a full port-map reconciliation. They are listed in **What I would do next** rather than presented as completed evidence.

### Example Windows checks

```powershell
ipconfig /all
ping <vlan-default-gateway>
tracert <destination-ip>
Test-NetConnection <destination-ip> -Port <required-tcp-port>
arp -a
```

`ipconfig /all` verifies addressing and DNS. `ping` checks reachability, subject to ICMP filtering. `tracert` shows the path, though intermediate devices may not respond. `Test-NetConnection` checks the TCP port the application actually uses. `arp -a` shows the local cache.

A failed ping can mean ICMP is filtered, not that the path is broken. That is why the application-level check is the one that counts.

## Outcomes

The office now has four purpose-specific segments instead of one flat network. Each has a documented role, a consistent addressing convention and a place in the firewall policy. Management interfaces are separated from ordinary user traffic, and a new device has a defined home.

What the project gives me is not a headline number. It is a network that can be explained, changed and troubleshoot one segment at a time.
